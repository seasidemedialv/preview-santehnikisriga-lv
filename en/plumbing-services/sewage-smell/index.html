What you can check yourself first

Not every sewage smell needs a plumber. The most common cause is simple, and you can check it yourself in a few minutes.

1

Find a rarely used drain

A floor drain, a guest toilet, or a sink you barely use - that's where the trap seal dries out most often.

2

Pour in a glass of water

Pour water into every rarely used drain and wait a couple of hours.

3

Smell still there? Send the form

If the smell persists after adding water, the cause runs deeper - you'll need professional diagnosis.

Important: Sewer gases can be harmful to health in high concentrations. If the smell is very strong or you feel unwell, ventilate the room and contact us right away.

Most common causes of sewage smell

Our plumbers diagnose the exact cause before starting work - not just offer a generic drain cleaning

Dried-out trap seal

The most common cause in rarely used drains and floor drains

Damaged seal

Worn rubber gasket at the base of a toilet or at pipe connections

Venting problem

A blocked or incorrectly installed roof vent pipe

Cracked pipe

A damaged or cracked drain pipe under the floor or behind a wall

Partial blockage

An early build-up of grease or debris that doesn't yet stop drainage

Incorrectly installed plumbing

A fitting connected wrong or without sealant during a past renovation

Why diagnosis matters, not just masking the smell

Many people try to solve sewage smell with air fresheners or scented sprays - that masks the problem for a while but doesn't fix the cause. A dried-out trap seal is the simplest case and often fixable yourself. A damaged seal or a cracked pipe needs professional diagnosis and repair - ignored for long, it can get worse and cause moisture damage. Our plumber never starts with a ready-made solution - first we diagnose the real cause, explain what happened, and only then offer a fix with a clear price.

Air admittance valve: when it helps

If the drains gurgle or the smell appears exactly when you flush the toilet or empty the bath, the cause may be a lack of air in the system. Water flowing through the pipe creates negative pressure and sucks the water out of the traps, and the smell comes into the room through the empty trap.

An air admittance valve (also called an aerator) prevents this: it opens when negative pressure builds up in the pipe, lets air in and closes once the flow stops. No smell escapes through it. It is fitted on a long branch, where several fixtures share one pipe, or where there is no vent.

  • The valve must sit higher than the trap it protects and stay accessible, because it needs checking now and then.
  • The valve does not replace the venting of the building's riser; if the whole building gurgles, that is a matter for the house manager.
  • If only one sink gurgles, first check whether the pipe is partly blocked.
